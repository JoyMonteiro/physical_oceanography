Lecture: The Mixed Layer - Turbulence, Diffusion, and Eddy Viscosity

1. Introduction: The Observational Puzzle and Turbulence

If we look at the surface of the ocean, we see a system that is constantly being forced. Gradients should be generated continuously because you have the sun heating the surface, rain altering the salinity, and winds dragging against the water. Yet, observations reveal a striking feature: the top layer of the ocean—the mixed layer—has virtually no gradients of anything. Whether it is energy, momentum, or chemical tracers, everything is uniformly distributed.

Any gradients that the surface forcing tries to create are destroyed by something that is very quickly mixing the water. This is largely due to kinetic energy being generated by the winds. Winds continuously hit the surface, transferring momentum and generating kinetic energy. Because fluid dynamics are inherently non-linear, energy input at a specific spatial or temporal scale starts spreading out, creating a whole variety of scales. This energy cascade generates classic fluid eddies, resulting in massive turbulence.

We know that in the ocean, molecular viscosity simply does not matter. To formalize our ideas and develop the technical machinery to analyze this (or any other fluid problem), we will apply our standard approach: find the equations, find the typical magnitudes, define the non-dimensional numbers, see which numbers matter, and simplify the equations accordingly.

2. The Limits of Molecular Viscosity

In our recent discussions on the Navier-Stokes equations and non-dimensionalization, we explored the balance of forces using dimensionless numbers. Recall the Reynolds number ($Re$), which scales the inertial forces against viscous forces:

$$Re = \frac{U L}{\nu}$$

where $U$ is a characteristic velocity, $L$ is a characteristic length scale, and $\nu$ is the kinematic molecular viscosity.

For the ocean, using typical basin scales ($L \sim 10^6$ m) and velocities ($U \sim 0.1$ m/s), and given the molecular viscosity of seawater ($\nu \sim 10^{-6}$ m$^2$/s), we find $Re \sim 10^{11}$. This is a massively turbulent regime. Yet, when we observe large-scale ocean properties—like the poleward transport of heat—the ocean behaves as if it has a much higher "effective" viscosity. To understand why, we must derive the diffusion equation from fundamental stresses and see how turbulence alters the picture.

3. Deriving the Diffusion Equation from Shear Stress

Let us start with the rigorous, yet straightforward, molecular picture. Consider a 1D vertical column of fluid where the horizontal velocity $u$ varies with depth $z$.

The Momentum Flux (Viscosity)

Adjacent layers of fluid exert a frictional force on one another. According to Newton's law of viscosity, the shear stress $\tau$ (force per unit area) across a horizontal plane is proportional to the vertical velocity gradient:

$$\tau = \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}$$

where $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid. Physically, $\tau$ represents the vertical flux of horizontal momentum.

To find the net force on a fluid parcel of thickness $\delta z$, we look at the difference in stress between the top and bottom of the parcel. The net force per unit volume is the vertical divergence of this stress:

$$F_{viscous} = \frac{\partial \tau}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \right)$$

Assuming $\mu$ is constant, and dividing by density $\rho$, we get the acceleration due to viscosity:

$$\frac{1}{\rho} F_{viscous} = \frac{\mu}{\rho} \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2}$$

If viscosity is the only force acting on the fluid, the momentum equation reduces to the diffusion equation for velocity:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2}$$

The Tracer Flux (Diffusion)

By exact analogy, we can look at a scalar tracer like temperature or salinity, which we will call $C$. Fick's First Law states that the molecular flux $J$ of this tracer is proportional to its concentration gradient:

$$J = -D \frac{\partial C}{\partial z}$$

where $D$ is the molecular diffusivity. To conserve mass (or heat), the rate of change of the tracer in a parcel must equal the negative divergence of the flux:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( D \frac{\partial C}{\partial z} \right)$$

Assuming $D$ is constant, we arrive at the standard diffusion equation:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial z^2}$$

Physical Meaning: The diffusion equation states that a property will change in time locally if there is a curvature (second derivative) in its spatial distribution. It naturally smooths out sharp gradients over time.

4. The Random Walk Perspective: A Unifying Physical Intuition

So far, we have derived these equations by treating the ocean as a continuous medium and relying on macroscopic empirical rules. But why do fundamentally different properties—momentum (scaled by $\nu$) and a chemical tracer (scaled by $D$)—obey the exact same mathematical equation?

To understand this, we zoom in and view the fluid through the statistical lens of a random walk. Imagine the ocean not as a continuum, but as a vast collection of particles undergoing continuous, random collisions.

  • For a tracer (like salt): A molecule randomly steps up, down, left, or right.
  • For momentum: A water molecule carrying the "memory" of the horizontal velocity of its current layer randomly wanders into an adjacent vertical layer, colliding and transferring its momentum. In this view, viscosity is simply the diffusion of momentum.

Because these countless "kicks" are independent and completely random, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that the resulting step sizes naturally form a normal (Gaussian) distribution. The expected net displacement of any single step is zero ($\mathbb{E}[\delta z_i] = 0$), but the variance (the expected squared displacement) is non-zero: $\mathbb{E}[(\delta z_i)^2] = (\Delta z)^2$.

When we calculate the total spread after $N$ independent steps, all the cross-terms in the expectation operator cancel out perfectly. We are left strictly with the sum of the individual variances:

$$\text{Spread Area (Variance)} = \mathbb{E}[Z_N^2] = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[(\delta z_i)^2] = N (\Delta z)^2$$

We know that the number of steps $N$ is the total time elapsed ($t$) divided by the time for one typical step ($\Delta t$). Substituting $N = t / \Delta t$:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z_N^2] = t \left( \frac{(\Delta z)^2}{\Delta t} \right)$$

This shows that the variance of the spread grows strictly linearly with time. The constant rate at which this variance expands is exactly what we define as the diffusivity coefficient:

$$D \text{ (or } \nu \text{)} \sim \frac{(\Delta z)^2}{\Delta t}$$

Deeper Insight: Whenever a property is transported by random, uncoordinated motions—whether heat, salt, or momentum—it will dynamically obey the diffusion equation.

5. The Reality of the Ocean: Reynolds Averaging & The Closure Problem

Using molecular diffusivity ($D \sim 10^{-9}$ m$^2$/s) to explain the ocean's mixing would imply it takes tens of thousands of years to mix a 100-meter layer. This contradicts our observations of the zero-gradient mixed layer.

To make our equations useful for a high-Reynolds-number ocean, we separate the fluid flow into a slow, large-scale "mean" flow and a fast, small-scale "turbulent" flow. This is known as Reynolds Decomposition:

$$w = \bar{w} + w' \quad \text{and} \quad C = \bar{C} + C'$$

By definition, the time average of the fluctuations is zero ($\overline{w'} = 0$). However, if we insert these into the advection equation for the tracer and take the time average, the non-linear term creates something profound:

$$\overline{w C} = \overline{(\bar{w} + w')(\bar{C} + C')} = \bar{w}\bar{C} + \overline{w'C'}$$

The time-evolution of the mean concentration $\bar{C}$ becomes:

$$\frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial t} + \bar{w} \frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial z} = D \frac{\partial^2 \bar{C}}{\partial z^2} - \frac{\partial}{\partial z} (\overline{w'C'})$$

The term $\overline{w'C'}$ is the Reynolds Flux (or eddy flux). Physically, it represents the transport of the tracer by turbulent eddies. We now face a Closure Problem: we have a new unknown, but no new equation for it.

We close the system using Gradient Transport Theory. We assume macroscopic turbulent eddies mix fluid analogously to microscopic molecular diffusion:

$$\overline{w'C'} = -K_z \frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial z}$$

Here, $K_z$ is the Eddy Diffusivity. Substituting this back (and ignoring the tiny molecular $D$), we get the Eddy Diffusion Equation:

$$\frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( K_z \frac{\partial \bar{C}}{\partial z} \right)$$

Using Prandtl's mixing length theory, an eddy travels a characteristic distance $l'$ with velocity $w'$ before dissolving. Plugging these into our random walk formula yields $K_z \sim w' l'$. Typical values for vertical eddy diffusivity are $K_z \sim 10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ m$^2$/s (thousands of times larger than molecular), successfully explaining the rapid formation of the zero-gradient mixed layer.

6. Scaling Analysis: When Does Rotation Matter?

In our technical machinery, we compare magnitudes of terms to identify dominant physical regimes. Let's compare the scale of the Coriolis acceleration to the vertical Eddy Diffusion acceleration:

  • Coriolis Acceleration: $\sim f U$
  • Vertical Diffusion: $\sim K_z \frac{U}{H^2}$

To find the regime where diffusion and rotation are equally important, we equate them:

$$f U \sim K_z \frac{U}{H^2} \implies H \sim \sqrt{\frac{K_z}{f}}$$

The dimensionless ratio $\frac{K_z}{f H^2}$ is the Ekman number ($Ek$). Interestingly, if we take the ratio of the Rossby number ($Ro = U / fL$) to the Reynolds number ($Re = UL / K$), the inertial terms perfectly cancel out:

$$\frac{Ro}{Re} = \frac{U / (f L)}{U L / K} = \frac{K}{f L^2} = Ek$$

The depth scale $H \sim \sqrt{K_z/f}$ tells us the vertical extent of the rotationally-influenced boundary layer. For typical mid-latitude values ($K_z \sim 10^{-2}$ m$^2$/s, $f \sim 10^{-4}$ s$^{-1}$), we find $H \sim 10$ meters. At depths shallower than this, turbulent mixing is so incredibly fast that the fluid behaves like a non-rotating system. As we reach $H$, the mixing timescale matches the planetary rotation timescale, and the Coriolis force fundamentally alters the physics (foreshadowing the Ekman spiral).

7. Scaling Analysis: Eddy Diffusion vs. Inertial Advection

Over what scales does a turbulent eddy dominate mixing before the mean flow simply carries it away? We compare mean advection to horizontal eddy diffusion ($K_h$):

  • Inertia (Advection): $\sim \frac{U^2}{L}$
  • Eddy Diffusion: $\sim K_h \frac{U}{L^2}$

Equating these yields the Mixing Length Scale ($L_{mix}$) and Mixing Time Scale ($T_{mix}$):

$$L_{mix} \sim \frac{K_h}{U} \quad \text{and} \quad T_{mix} \sim \frac{K_h}{U^2}$$

Using typical ocean values ($K_h \sim 10^4$ m$^2$/s, $U \sim 0.1$ m/s), we find $L_{mix} \sim 100$ km and $T_{mix} \sim 11.5$ days. This perfectly matches the spatial scale and lifespan of a typical synoptic mesoscale ocean eddy!

Lecture: Inertial Oscillations - From Rotational Balance to Damped Trajectories

1. Introduction: A Push in a Rotating World

In our previous discussions, we explored how the ocean reaches a steady state, balancing forces like friction and rotation to form Ekman layers, or balancing pressure gradients and rotation to form geostrophic currents. But what happens to a fluid parcel when it is suddenly pushed—perhaps by a passing hurricane or a sharp gust of wind—and then left alone?

Without continuous wind to drive it, and without an immediate pressure gradient to steer it, the fluid parcel does not simply stop, nor does it travel in a straight line. On a rotating planet, its "straight line" momentum is constantly hijacked by the Coriolis force. This leads to one of the most ubiquitous, mesmerising, and transient phenomena in the ocean: the Inertial Oscillation.

To understand this, we must return to the Navier-Stokes equations, strip away the complexities using non-dimensional numbers, and isolate the pure physics of inertia and rotation.

2. Regime Identification via Non-Dimensional Numbers

Let us start with the horizontal momentum equations on a rotating Earth (the $f$-plane approximation), including local acceleration, non-linear advection, the Coriolis force, pressure gradients, and viscous friction:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} - fv = -\frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \nu \nabla^2 u$$
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + u \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + fu = -\frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} + \nu \nabla^2 v$$

To isolate an inertial oscillation, we must ask: what physical regime allows a fluid parcel to simply "coast" under the influence of rotation? We establish this regime by evaluating our dimensionless numbers.

Linearization and the Rossby Number ($Ro$):
To rigorously justify simplifying the equations, we must compare the two types of acceleration acting on the fluid: local acceleration ($\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$) and non-linear advective acceleration ($u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$).

  • Local acceleration scales as $U/T$, where $T$ is the characteristic time scale of the motion.
  • Advective acceleration scales as $U^2/L$, where $L$ is the characteristic spatial length scale.

The ratio of advective to local acceleration is:

$$\frac{\text{Advection}}{\text{Local Acceleration}} \sim \frac{U^2 / L}{U / T} = \frac{U T}{L}$$

For phenomena dominated by rotation, the natural time scale is the inverse of the Coriolis parameter, $T \sim 1/f$. Substituting this into our ratio yields the Rossby number ($Ro$):

$$Ro = \frac{U}{fL}$$

When a wind gust triggers an inertial oscillation, it typically generates small-amplitude velocities ($U$) over very broad expanses of the open ocean ($L$). Under these conditions, $U \ll fL$, meaning $Ro \ll 1$.

This small Rossby number formally justifies linearization. It proves that the non-linear advective terms ($U^2/L$) are vanishingly small compared to the local acceleration ($U/T$) and the Coriolis force ($fU$). We can therefore safely drop $u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ and its counterparts from our equations, leaving the motion fundamentally linear.

The Ekman Number ($Ek$):
As derived in our last lecture, the Ekman number scales frictional viscous forces against the Coriolis force:

$$Ek = \frac{\nu}{f H^2}$$

In the interior ocean, away from the immediate sticky boundaries of the surface and the sea floor, friction is negligible over short timescales. Therefore, $Ek \ll 1$, and we can drop the viscous terms ($\nu \nabla^2 u$).

The Unforced, Unpressurized Assumption:
If the wind event has passed (no external forcing) and we are looking at a localized region of the open ocean where the sea surface is relatively flat, the horizontal pressure gradients ($\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$) are practically zero.

The Resulting Balance:
What remains? Only the local rate of change of velocity (pure inertia) and the Coriolis force. The equations dramatically simplify to:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - fv = 0$$
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + fu = 0$$

Physical Meaning: These simplified equations represent a pure balance between the local acceleration of the fluid and the Earth's rotation. There is no friction to slow the parcel down, no pressure to push it down a gradient, and no non-linear shear to tear it apart.

3. The Solution: Circles in the Sea

We are left with a system of two coupled, first-order linear ordinary differential equations. To solve them, we can differentiate the first equation with respect to time:

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - f \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = 0$$

Substitute the second equation ($\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = -fu$) into this:

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} + f^2 u = 0$$

This is the classic equation for a simple harmonic oscillator! The solution is a combination of sines and cosines. Assuming that at time $t=0$, the parcel was given an initial push purely in the x-direction with velocity $U_0$ (so $u(0) = U_0$ and $v(0) = 0$), the velocities over time are:

$$u(t) = U_0 \cos(ft)$$
$$v(t) = -U_0 \sin(ft)$$

To find the actual trajectory of the fluid parcel, we integrate the velocities with respect to time to get the positions $x(t)$ and $y(t)$:

$$x(t) = \int u(t) dt = \frac{U_0}{f} \sin(ft) + x_0$$
$$y(t) = \int v(t) dt = \frac{U_0}{f} \cos(ft) - \frac{U_0}{f} + y_0$$

If we set the starting position to the origin ($x_0 = 0, y_0 = U_0/f$ for algebraic neatness), we can square both equations and add them together:

$$x^2 + y^2 = \left( \frac{U_0}{f} \right)^2 [\sin^2(ft) + \cos^2(ft)] = \left( \frac{U_0}{f} \right)^2$$

Physical Meaning: The trajectory $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$ is a perfect circle! A fluid parcel given a push on a rotating planet will coast in a continuous circle. Because $f$ is positive in the Northern Hemisphere, the equations dictate that the parcel turns constantly to the right, tracing a clockwise circle. In the Southern Hemisphere ($f$ is negative), it turns left, tracing a counter-clockwise circle.

4. Quantitative Estimates: Putting Numbers to the Physics

Let us see if these theoretical "inertial circles" make sense in the real ocean. We need two parameters: the inertial radius ($R$) and the inertial period ($T$).

From our solution, the radius of the circle is:

$$R = \frac{U_0}{f}$$

The time it takes to complete one full circle is determined by the angular frequency $f$. The period $T$ is:

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{f}$$

Let's plug in typical mid-latitude ocean values:

  • Coriolis parameter at 45° latitude: $f \sim 10^{-4} \text{ s}^{-1}$
  • Initial velocity from a strong wind gust: $U_0 \sim 0.5 \text{ m/s}$

The Inertial Radius ($R$):

$$R = \frac{0.5 \text{ m/s}}{10^{-4} \text{ s}^{-1}} = 5000 \text{ meters} = 5 \text{ km}$$

The Inertial Period ($T$):

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{10^{-4} \text{ s}^{-1}} \approx 62,800 \text{ seconds} \approx 17.4 \text{ hours}$$

Physical Check: This perfectly matches observations! If you drop a GPS-tracked drifter buoy in the ocean after a storm, you will routinely see it looping in tight circles of about 5 to 10 kilometers wide, taking roughly three-quarters of a day to complete one loop.

5. Modifications: Mean Flow and Eddy Diffusion

Our pure inertial oscillation assumed a motionless background and a frictionless fluid. In reality, the ocean is messy. Let's re-introduce two vital components.

A. The Presence of a Mean Flow

What if the parcel is oscillating while embedded in a large-scale, steady background current (like the Gulf Stream)?
Let the mean flow have a constant velocity $U_{mean}$ in the x-direction. The velocity of our parcel simply becomes a superposition of the mean flow and the oscillation:

$$u(t) = U_{mean} + U_0 \cos(ft)$$
$$v(t) = -U_0 \sin(ft)$$

Integrating for the trajectory gives:

$$x(t) = U_{mean} t + \frac{U_0}{f} \sin(ft)$$
$$y(t) = \frac{U_0}{f} \cos(ft)$$

Physical Meaning: The trajectory is no longer a closed circle. It becomes a cycloid (a looping, spring-like path). The parcel loops around, but gets carried downstream by the mean flow. If $U_{mean}$ is very strong compared to $U_0$, the loops stretch out into a wavy, meandering line.

B. The Presence of Eddy Diffusion (Friction)

Inertial oscillations do not last forever; the kinetic energy injected by the storm eventually dissipates. In our previous lecture, we established that turbulent eddies act like a massive "effective viscosity," parameterized by an eddy diffusivity $K_z$.

If we re-introduce a simple parameterization for friction—a linear drag term directly proportional to velocity ($-r u$, where $r$ is a friction coefficient)—our momentum equations become:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - fv = -r u$$
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + fu = -r v$$

Solving this modified system yields:

$$u(t) = U_0 e^{-rt} \cos(ft)$$
$$v(t) = -U_0 e^{-rt} \sin(ft)$$

Physical Meaning: The $e^{-rt}$ term is an exponential decay envelope. The fluid parcel still tries to complete its inertial circles, but friction bleeds away its kinetic energy over time. Instead of a closed circle, the trajectory becomes an inward-spiraling path, eventually grinding to a halt.

Deeper Insight: In a fully stratified 3D ocean, this decay isn't just local friction turning into heat. The alternating shear of the inertial oscillation actually generates near-inertial internal waves. The energy of the surface oscillation is physically radiated downward into the deep ocean, providing a crucial source of energy to drive deep turbulent mixing!

6. Summary of Inertial Oscillations

  • Non-Dimensional Scaling & Linearization: By comparing advection to local acceleration ($U^2/L$ vs $U/T$), we derive the Rossby number ($Ro \sim U/fL$). For small-amplitude motions over large scales, $Ro \ll 1$, formally justifying the linearization of our equations.
  • The Solution: Combined with a low Ekman number, this pure balance creates a harmonic oscillator. The fluid traces perfect circular trajectories, coasting clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counter-clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • Quantitative Scales: For a typical mid-latitude wind event ($U_0 \sim 0.5$ m/s), the inertial radius is roughly 5 km, and the period is about 17.5 hours.
  • Mean Flow Modification: Superimposing a background current transforms the closed circular trajectories into open, looping cycloids.
  • Diffusion/Friction Modification: Adding turbulent eddy friction causes the oscillations to damp out exponentially over time, resulting in inward-spiraling trajectories and the downward radiation of internal wave energy.
