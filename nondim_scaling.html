Physical Oceanography Class Notes

Non-Dimensionalization, Scaling, and Similarity

From Ocean Mixing to the Navier-Stokes Equations

1. Motivation: The Physics of Mixing

Why do we obsess over algebra and dimensionless numbers? To answer this, let us look at the ocean. Consider the transport of energy. In the tropics, we have significant heating—water vapor and heat accumulation. Observations show that this heat slowly moves poleward. If we model this as energy transport, we can define a diffusivity for the atmosphere or the ocean.

Imagine a vortex or an eddy bringing hot fluid from one side and cold fluid from the other. The fundamental question is: Over what length scale does that mixing happen?

If you have very small eddies versus a large eddy, the physics changes. A large eddy mixes temperature over a large length scale. This concept is the "mixing length scale" or "mixing time scale." It tells us something fundamental about the system's disturbances—what phenomena can actually exist? Even though non-dimensionalization looks like "just a bunch of algebra," it leads to physical insights into the fundamental nature of the flow, allowing us to compare a cup of coffee to an ocean current.

2. The Buckingham Pi Theorem

Before deriving equations, we often use the Buckingham Pi theorem to determine relevant parameters without knowing the exact governing equation. The theorem states:

The Theorem: If a problem is described by \( k \) physical variables involving \( p \) fundamental dimensions (Mass \( M \), Length \( L \), Time \( T \), Temperature \( \Theta \)), we can reduce the problem to a set of \( n = k - p \) dimensionless parameters, denoted as \( \Pi_1, \Pi_2, \dots, \Pi_n \).

Physical Significance: This reduction is not merely mathematical convenience. It is the basis of Similitude. It allows us to design scale models (wind tunnels, water tanks) where the physics matches the full-scale prototype, provided the \( \Pi \) groups (like Reynolds number) match.

3. Scaling vs. Non-Dimensionalization: The Simple Pendulum

We will now perform the rigorous derivation step-by-step. The goal is to transform our dimensional equation into a fundamental form by explicitly defining reference scales.

Consider the governing equation for a simple pendulum of length \( L \) and mass \( m \), where \( \phi \) is the angle:

$$ \frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2} + \frac{g}{L}\sin(\phi) = 0 $$

Computational Example: Why Mass Doesn't Matter

We can use Linear Algebra and Python to automatically find the "Null Space" of the dimensional matrix. This purely algebraic approach confirms that mass ($m$) must vanish from the time-scale relationship.

Below is the SymPy code that sets up the dimensional matrix for variables $[m, L, g, T]$. Notice how the null space vector for mass is strictly zero. 

import sympy
from sympy import Matrix

# 1. Define the Dimensional Matrix
# Rows: Fundamental Dimensions [Mass, Length, Time]
# Columns: Physical Variables  [m,    L,     g,    T_period]

# m (mass)   = M^1 L^0 T^0
# L (length) = M^0 L^1 T^0
# g (gravity)= M^0 L^1 T^-2
# T (period) = M^0 L^0 T^1

dimensional_matrix = Matrix([
    [1, 0, 0, 0],   # Mass row
    [0, 1, 1, 0],   # Length row
    [0, 0, -2, 1]   # Time row
])

# 2. Compute the Null Space (Kernel)
# The null space vectors represent the exponents of the Pi groups.
null_space = dimensional_matrix.nullspace()

print("Null Space Basis Vectors:")
for vec in null_space:
    print(vec)

# Output Calculation:
# The solver finds a vector v = [e_m, e_L, e_g, e_T] such that Matrix * v = 0.
# Row 1 (Mass) implies: 1 * e_m = 0  ---> e_m MUST be 0.
# This proves mathematically that the period T cannot depend on mass m.

Step 1: Systematic Selection of Scales

We begin by decomposing our variables into a Reference Scale (with units) and a Non-dimensional Variable (magnitude ~ 1).

Time Scaling: Let \( T_R \) be our reference time scale.

$$ t = T_R \cdot t^* $$

Angle Scaling: Let \( \Phi_R \) be our reference angle scale.

$$ \phi = \Phi_R \cdot \phi^* $$

Here, \( t^* \) and \( \phi^* \) are unitless. The derivative terms scale as: $$ \frac{d}{dt} = \frac{1}{T_R}\frac{d}{dt^*} \quad \implies \quad \frac{d^2}{dt^2} = \frac{1}{T_R^2}\frac{d^2}{dt^{*2}} $$

Step 2: Substitution and Balancing

Substituting these into the governing equation:

$$ \frac{\Phi_R}{T_R^2} \frac{d^2\phi^*}{dt^{*2}} + \frac{g}{L}\sin(\Phi_R \phi^*) = 0 $$

To identify the natural frequency, we balance the inertial term (acceleration) with the gravitational term. Dividing the entire equation by the coefficient \( \Phi_R/T_R^2 \):

$$ \frac{d^2\phi^*}{dt^{*2}} + \left[ \frac{g T_R^2}{L} \right] \frac{\sin(\Phi_R \phi^*)}{\Phi_R} = 0 $$

For the physics to be invariant, the bracketed term must be of order unity (specifically 1). This defines our reference time scale \( T_R \):

$$ \frac{g T_R^2}{L} = 1 \implies T_R = \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} $$

Step 3: Choosing the Reference Angle \(\Phi_R\)

The choice of \( \Phi_R \) depends entirely on the initial conditions of the system. This highlights a key subtlety in scaling: the "correct" scale depends on the regime of the physics.

Case A: Release from Rest (Small/Moderate Amplitude)

If the pendulum is released from an initial angle \( \phi_0 \) with zero velocity, the natural scale for the motion is that initial angle itself.

$$ \Phi_R = \phi_0 $$

The dimensionless initial condition becomes \( \phi^*(0) = 1 \). If \( \phi_0 \) is small, the sine term linearizes (\( \sin(\phi_0 \phi^*) \approx \phi_0 \phi^* \)), recovering the simple harmonic oscillator.

Case B: General Motion (Initial Angle + Velocity)

Consider a more general case where the pendulum starts at an angle \( \phi_0 \) and has an initial angular velocity \( d\phi/dt = \Omega_0 \).

To find the appropriate scale \( \Phi_R \), we look at the Total Energy (Kinetic + Potential). The initial energy is: $$ E_{total} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} m (L \Omega_0)^2}_{\text{Kinetic}} + \underbrace{m g L (1 - \cos \phi_0)}_{\text{Potential}} $$

We define the reference scale \( \Phi_R \) as the maximum amplitude \( \Phi_{max} \). At this peak, velocity is zero, so all energy is potential: $$ E_{total} = m g L (1 - \cos \Phi_{max}) $$

Equating the two yields the definition for our scale: $$ m g L (1 - \cos \Phi_{max}) = \frac{1}{2} m L^2 \Omega_0^2 + m g L (1 - \cos \phi_0) $$

The Singularity (Impulsive Start)

This general energy method (even the simplified one) resolves the "singularity" of the impulsive start. If we had tried to use \( \Phi_R = \phi_0 \) when \( \phi_0 = 0 \) (starting at the bottom with a kick), our dimensionless variable \( \phi^* = \phi/\phi_0 \) would blow up to infinity.

However, using the energy equation above with \( \phi_0 = 0 \), we get a well-defined \( \Phi_{max} \) driven purely by the kinetic term \( \frac{1}{2} m L^2 \Omega_0^2 \). This confirms that energy is the robust scaling choice for high-speed or mixed regimes.

Step 4: The Universal Non-Dimensional Equation

Regardless of the specific choice of \( \Phi_R \), the time-scaled equation collapses the parameters \( g \) and \( L \) out of the dynamics completely:

$$ \frac{d^2\phi^*}{d\tau^2} + \sin(\phi^*) = 0 \quad (\text{using } \Phi_R=1 \text{ for simplicity}) $$

4. Transition: The Diffusion Equation & Mixing Length

Before we jump to the complexity of the Navier-Stokes equations, let’s look at a simpler, linear partial differential equation (PDE) that directly relates to our motivation of ocean mixing: the Diffusion Equation. This derivation will make the concept of "mixing length scale" concrete.

Step 1: Deriving the Equation

We start with the principle of Conservation of Mass (or Energy). For a tracer concentration \( C \), the rate of change in a volume is equal to the net flux coming in or out.

$$ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 $$

Here, \( \mathbf{J} \) is the flux vector. We apply Fick's Law (or Fourier's Law for heat), which states that flux moves from high to low concentration:

$$ \mathbf{J} = - \kappa \nabla C $$

Substituting this back into the conservation law gives us the Diffusion Equation:

$$ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \kappa \nabla^2 C $$

Step 2: Scaling Analysis & The Mixing Length

Now, let's apply the same scaling technique we used for the pendulum. We introduce characteristic scales:

  • \( \mathcal{T} \): Characteristic Time Scale (e.g., lifespan of an eddy)
  • \( \mathcal{L} \): Characteristic Length Scale (Mixing Length)

Substituting \( t = \mathcal{T} t^* \) and \( x = \mathcal{L} x^* \) into the 1D diffusion equation:

$$ \frac{C_0}{\mathcal{T}} \frac{\partial C^*}{\partial t^*} = \kappa \frac{C_0}{\mathcal{L}^2} \frac{\partial^2 C^*}{\partial x^{*2}} $$

Dividing by the time coefficient \( C_0 / \mathcal{T} \):

$$ \frac{\partial C^*}{\partial t^*} = \left[ \frac{\kappa \mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{L}^2} \right] \frac{\partial^2 C^*}{\partial x^{*2}} $$

For the physics to be balanced (invariant), the term in brackets must be of order unity. This gives us the fundamental definition of the Mixing Length Scale:

$$ \frac{\kappa \mathcal{T}}{\mathcal{L}^2} \sim 1 \implies \mathcal{L} \sim \sqrt{\kappa \mathcal{T}} $$
Physical Interpretation: This result (\( \mathcal{L} \sim \sqrt{\kappa \mathcal{T}} \)) tells us that if we observe a tracer diffusing for a time \( \mathcal{T} \) with diffusivity \( \kappa \), the "patch size" or distance it has traveled is proportional to the square root of time.

In the context of the ocean motivation: If we know the eddy diffusivity \( \kappa \) and the turnover time of an eddy \( \mathcal{T} \), we can immediately predict the size of the water mass \( \mathcal{L} \) that will be mixed.

5. Application to Navier-Stokes

Moving to fluid mechanics, we apply this rigorous process to the Navier-Stokes equations. We have 3 dimensions but 7 quantities, leading to 4 dimensionless numbers.

The dimensional momentum equation for incompressible flow is:

$$ \rho \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u} \right) = -\nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \rho \mathbf{g} $$

We introduce characteristic scales: Length \( L \), Velocity \( U \), Density \( \rho \), and Frequency \( \omega \) (or time \( T \)). Substituting these into the equation and dividing by the inertial scale \( \rho U^2 / L \), we obtain the non-dimensional Navier-Stokes equation:

$$ St \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}^*}{\partial t^*} + (\mathbf{u}^* \cdot \nabla^*) \mathbf{u}^* = -Eu \nabla^* p^* + \frac{1}{Re} \nabla^{*2} \mathbf{u}^* + \frac{1}{Fr^2} \mathbf{g}^* $$

The Non-Dimensional Groups

Reynolds Number (Re): $$ Re = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} = \frac{\text{Inertial Forces}}{\text{Viscous Forces}} $$

Determines if flow is laminar or turbulent.

Froude Number (Fr): $$ Fr = \frac{U}{\sqrt{gL}} = \frac{\text{Inertial Forces}}{\text{Gravitational Forces}} $$

Critical in free-surface flows (waves).

Euler Number (Eu): $$ Eu = \frac{\Delta p}{\rho U^2} = \frac{\text{Pressure Forces}}{\text{Inertial Forces}} $$

Relates pressure drop to dynamic head.

Strouhal Number (St): $$ St = \frac{\omega L}{U} = \frac{\text{Oscillating Flow}}{\text{Mean Flow}} $$

Important for unsteady, oscillating flows.

In rotating systems, we would introduce the Coriolis term, leading to the Rossby Number ($Ro = U / fL$).
