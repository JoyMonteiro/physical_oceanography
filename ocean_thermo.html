Part 2: Comprehensive Lecture Notes

I. Introduction: The Unclosed System

In our previous discussion (Jan 16), we established the Boussinesq equations of motion. However, this system is mathematically "open."

Class Discussion: In standard continuum mechanics or ideal gas dynamics, we are primarily interested in conservation of momentum and mass. Often, systems in those fields can be closed with the simplest possible relationship: $\rho = \text{constant}$. This trivial closure allows one to solve the system without invoking detailed thermodynamics. However, for the ocean, this simplification fails because density variations drive circulation. We typically refer to this relationship between density and other properties as the Equation of State. To make any progress in oceanography, unlike in simple fluid mechanics, we absolutely need to understand the thermodynamic nature of the fluid.

  • The Counting Problem:
    • Unknowns (5): Velocities ($u, v, w$), Pressure ($p$), Density ($\rho$).
    • Equations (4): Three momentum equations + One continuity equation ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$).
  • The Solution: We need an Equation of State (EOS): $\rho = \rho(S, T, p)$.
    • Note: In a barotropic fluid, $\rho = \rho(p)$, and we are done. In the baroclinic ocean, density depends on Temperature ($T$) and Salinity ($S$).
  • The Consequence: Introducing $T$ and $S$ adds two new unknowns. We must therefore derive evolution equations for them using thermodynamics.

II. Derivation of the Thermodynamic Energy Equation

Goal: Derive the evolution equation for temperature.

Step 1: The First Law of Thermodynamics

For a fluid parcel, the change in Internal Energy ($I$) equals heat added ($\dot{Q}$) minus work done by expansion ($p D\alpha/Dt$):

$$\frac{DI}{Dt} = \dot{Q} - p \frac{D\alpha}{Dt}$$

Step 2: The Fundamental Relation

Combining the First and Second Laws:

$$dI = T d\eta - p d\alpha + \mu dS$$

Taking the material derivative:

$$\frac{DI}{Dt} = T \frac{D\eta}{Dt} - p \frac{D\alpha}{Dt} + \mu \frac{DS}{Dt}$$

Step 3: The Entropy Equation

Equating Step 1 and Step 2:

$$\dot{Q} - p \frac{D\alpha}{Dt} = T \frac{D\eta}{Dt} - p \frac{D\alpha}{Dt} + \mu \frac{DS}{Dt}$$ $$T \frac{D\eta}{Dt} = \dot{Q} - \mu \frac{DS}{Dt}$$

Step 4: Transforming Entropy to Temperature

We measure $T$, not $\eta$. First, we expand $\frac{D\eta}{Dt}$ using the chain rule, assuming entropy is a function of Temperature ($T$), Pressure ($p$), and Salinity ($S$):

$$\frac{D\eta}{Dt} = \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial T} \right)_{p,S} \frac{DT}{Dt} + \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial p} \right)_{T,S} \frac{Dp}{Dt} + \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial S} \right)_{T,p} \frac{DS}{Dt}$$

We then substitute standard thermodynamic definitions (derived in later sections):

  • Specific Heat Capacity: $\left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial T} \right)_{p,S} = \frac{c_p}{T}$
  • Maxwell Relation: $\left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial p} \right)_{T,S} = -\alpha \beta$
  • (Note: Salinity effects are often handled separately or neglected in simplified thermal derivations).

Substituting these back yields the final temperature equation:

$$c_p \frac{DT}{Dt} = \dot{Q} + T \alpha \beta \frac{Dp}{Dt}$$
  • $\dot{Q}$: Diabatic heating (Solar/Mixing).
  • $T \alpha \beta Dp/Dt$: Adiabatic compression heating (why deep water warms up under pressure).

III. Thermodynamic Potentials & Legendre Transformations

To calculate coefficients like $\beta$ and $c_p$, we need a thermodynamic potential.

1. The Fundamental Relation ($I$):

$$dI = T d\eta - p d\alpha + \mu dS$$

Problem: Natural variables are $\eta$ (unmeasurable) and $\alpha$ (not a control variable).

2. Enthalpy ($h$): Swapping $\alpha \rightarrow p$

$$h = I + p\alpha \quad \Rightarrow \quad dh = T d\eta + \alpha dp + \mu dS$$

Use: Heat budgets ($dh = dQ$ at constant pressure).

3. Gibbs Free Energy ($g$): Swapping $\eta \rightarrow T$ and $\alpha \rightarrow p$

$$g = h - T\eta \quad \Rightarrow \quad dg = -\eta dT + \alpha dp + \mu dS$$

Use: The Standard for Oceanography. Its variables ($T, p, S$) are exactly what we measure.

IV. Maxwell's Relations

Before we can use the Gibbs function to calculate practical oceanographic variables, we need a mathematical tool to handle terms involving entropy.

1. The Mathematical Principle

Because $g(T, p, S)$ is a state function, its differential $dg$ is "exact." This implies that the mixed partial derivatives must be equal. The order of differentiation does not matter:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p} \left( \frac{\partial g}{\partial T} \right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial T} \left( \frac{\partial g}{\partial p} \right)$$

2. Deriving the Relation

From the Gibbs differential $dg = -\eta dT + \alpha dp + \mu dS$:

  • The derivative with respect to $T$ is $-\eta$.
  • The derivative with respect to $p$ is $\alpha$.

Substitute these into the equality above:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p} (-\eta)_T = \frac{\partial}{\partial T} (\alpha)_p$$

3. The Result (Maxwell's Relation for Gibbs)

$$-\left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial p} \right)_T = \left( \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial T} \right)_p$$

4. Physical Significance

  • Left Hand Side: How entropy changes with pressure. This is effectively impossible to measure directly in the ocean.
  • Right Hand Side: How specific volume changes with temperature. This is simply thermal expansion, which is easily measured in a lab.
  • Conclusion: Maxwell's relations allow us to replace unmeasurable entropy terms with measurable volume/density terms. This is the key step that makes the derivation of $c_v$ possible in the next section.

V. Utility: Deriving Quantities from the Gibbs Function

In modern oceanography (TEOS-10), we fit a single function $g(T,p,S)$ to data. All other properties are derived mathematically from this surface using the principles above.

1. Density ($\rho$)

From $dg = -\eta dT + \alpha dp$, the partial derivative with respect to pressure is volume:

$$\alpha = \left( \frac{\partial g}{\partial p} \right)_{T,S} \implies \rho = \frac{1}{g_p}$$

2. Thermal Expansion ($\beta$) and Compressibility ($\kappa_T$)

These coefficients describe how the ocean's volume changes. They are essential for stability analysis and sound speed calculations.

Thermal Expansion Coefficient ($\beta$):
Defined as the fractional change in volume with temperature at constant pressure.

$$\beta \equiv \frac{1}{\alpha} \left( \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial T} \right)_p = \frac{g_{pT}}{g_p}$$

Isothermal Compressibility ($\kappa_T$):
Defined as the fractional change in volume with pressure at constant temperature.

$$\kappa_T \equiv -\frac{1}{\alpha} \left( \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial p} \right)_T = -\frac{g_{pp}}{g_p}$$

3. Specific Heat at Constant Pressure ($c_p$)

Class Discussion: In class, we emphasized that $c_p$ is directly related to Ocean Heat Content, which is an important quantity in itself. To derive this, think about "Heat Provided." Since $dQ = T d\eta$, at constant pressure we can write $c_p dT = T d\eta$. This allows us to calculate $c_p$ if we know how to compute entropy $\eta$ from the Gibbs function. Through symbol manipulation of these thermodynamic identities, you should be able to get to any single thermodynamic quantity.

$$c_p \equiv T \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial T} \right)_p$$

Since $\eta = -g_T$, substituting gives:

$$c_p = -T g_{TT}$$

4. Specific Heat at Constant Volume ($c_v$)

We cannot measure $c_v$ in the ocean because specific volume ($\alpha$) changes with depth. Therefore, we must derive $c_v$ entirely in terms of the measurable Gibbs function $g(T, p)$.

Step A: The Definition

$$c_v \equiv T \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial T} \right)_\alpha$$

Note the subscript $\alpha$: Volume is held constant.

Step B: Changing Variables (from $\alpha$ to $p$)
Using partial calculus rules:

$$c_v = T \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial T} \right)_p + T \left( \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial p} \right)_T \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} \right)_\alpha$$

Step C: Substitution

  • First term is $c_p$.
  • Second term uses Maxwell's Relation (from Section IV): $\left(\frac{\partial \eta}{\partial p}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial T}\right)_p$.
  • Third term (Triple Product Rule): $\left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} \right)_\alpha = - \frac{(\partial \alpha / \partial T)_p}{(\partial \alpha / \partial p)_T}$.

Substituting:

$$c_v = c_p + T \left[ - \left( \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial T} \right)_p \right] \left[ - \frac{(\partial \alpha / \partial T)_p}{(\partial \alpha / \partial p)_T} \right]$$

Step D: Final Result with Gibbs Derivatives
Now replace terms with the definitions derived in Subsections 1 & 2:

  • $c_p = -T g_{TT}$
  • $(\partial \alpha / \partial T)_p = \alpha \beta = g_{pT}$
  • $(\partial \alpha / \partial p)_T = -\alpha \kappa_T = g_{pp}$
$$c_v = (-T g_{TT}) + T \frac{(g_{pT})^2}{g_{pp}}$$

Final Result:

$$c_v = -T \left( g_{TT} - \frac{(g_{pT})^2}{g_{pp}} \right)$$

VI. Physical Interpretation: $c_p$ vs. $c_v$

The difference between these two specific heats is given by the relation:

$$c_p - c_v = \frac{T \alpha \beta^2}{\kappa_T}$$

1. The Mechanism: Work of Expansion

Why is $c_p$ always larger than $c_v$?

  • Case $c_v$ (Fixed Volume): When you heat a fixed-volume parcel, it cannot move. 100% of the input energy goes directly into increasing molecular kinetic energy (raising temperature).
  • Case $c_p$ (Fixed Pressure): When you heat a parcel at constant pressure, it naturally wants to expand (governed by $\beta$). To expand, it must push back its boundaries against the surrounding fluid. This "pushing" requires mechanical work.
  • Conclusion: $c_p > c_v$ because you must supply extra heat to pay for this mechanical work, in addition to the energy needed to raise the temperature.

2. Comparison: Ideal Gas vs. The Ocean

  • Ideal Gas ($R$): For a gas, the intermolecular forces are negligible. The work done is purely against external pressure ($P \Delta V$). This leads to the famous relation $c_p - c_v = R$.
  • The Ocean (Complex Work): In water, molecules are packed tightly. The parcel must do work against external pressure AND against the strong internal attractive forces between water molecules.
    • This is why the RHS is not a constant like $R$. It depends on $\kappa_T$ (how stiff the fluid is) and $\beta$ (how much it tries to expand).

3. The "Cold Water Limit" (Oceanographic Special Case)

A unique feature of the ocean is that $\beta$ (thermal expansion) varies strongly and can even be zero.

  • At $4^\circ$C (Freshwater) or Freezing (Seawater): The thermal expansion coefficient $\beta$ approaches zero.
  • Consequence: If $\beta \approx 0$, then the RHS $\frac{T \alpha \beta^2}{\kappa_T} \approx 0$.
  • Result: $c_p \approx c_v$.
  • Physical Meaning: At these temperatures, adding heat causes no expansion. Since there is no expansion, no mechanical work is done against the surroundings. Therefore, the heat required to raise the temperature at constant pressure is exactly the same as at constant volume.
