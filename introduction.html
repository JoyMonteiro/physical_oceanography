The Evolution of Physical Oceanography

A synthesis of historical exploration, the Stewart PDF context, and modern dynamical framing (Lectures Jan 7 & 8).

I. Introduction & Sub-disciplines

Oceanography is fundamentally the study of the ocean as an environment. Unlike many other earth sciences, its evolution was driven by the practical need to "draw" or describe the ocean before it could be understood through physics. As noted in the Jan 7 lecture, the term Oceanography (meaning "to draw the ocean") persisted for a long time instead of "Oceanology," emphasizing its descriptive roots.

Primary Divisions:

  • Biological Oceanography: Historically the starting point of the discipline. Early scientists in Scandinavia and the Baltic Sea regions were driven by the fishery industry to understand plankton blooms.
  • Chemical Oceanography: Growing in importance due to global climate change, focusing on $CO_2$ emissions, isotope chemistry, and oxygen minimum zones (hypoxia).
  • Physical Oceanography: The study of the physical properties (temperature, salinity, density) and the dynamics (currents, waves, tides) of the ocean. This course focuses on this sub-discipline as a branch of Geophysics.
"Physical oceanographers used to be defined as people who go on ships, while dynamical oceanographers sat in labs with pen and paper." — Lecture Jan 7.

II. The Historical Setting

As Stewart notes in Chapter 2: The Historical Setting, our knowledge of the ocean goes back thousands of years, long before formal instruments. Those who "earned their living on the ocean" provided the first insights into currents and tides.

Ancient Knowledge

Knowledge of oceanic phenomena was critical for early trade and survival:

  • Polynesian Navigators (4000 BC): Traded over long distances in the Pacific using sophisticated star navigation and wave patterns.
  • The Samaveda (2000–1400 BC): Stewart highlights that this Indian Vedic text described the connection between tides and the positions of the sun and moon centuries before European science codified it.
  • Pytheas (325 BC): Explored the Atlantic from Italy to Norway, providing early records of the northern oceans.
  • Arabic Traders: Utilized the reversing monsoon winds and currents in the Indian Ocean to establish trade routes to China and Zanzibar during the Middle Ages.

The Age of Discovery (15th–16th Century)

Modern European knowledge began with voyages by Dias, Columbus, da Gama, and Magellan. These explorers laid the foundation for global trade by documenting the major wind systems—the Trade Winds and the Westerlies—and the western boundary currents that define the Atlantic and Pacific gyres.

III. The Seven Eras of Exploration

Stewart categorizes the history of oceanographic study into seven distinct eras, each marked by a shift in technology and intent.

1. Era of Surface Oceanography (Early times–1873)

Driven by mariners' observations of winds, currents, and surface temperature. Key figures included Benjamin Franklin, who mapped the Gulf Stream (1769) to speed up mail delivery, and Matthew Fontaine Maury, who systematized ship logs.

2. Era of Deep-Sea Exploration (1873–1914)

The HMS Challenger Expedition (1872–1876) is the defining event of this era. It was the first truly global scientific expedition, sampling the biology, chemistry, and physics of the deep ocean at hundreds of stations.

3. Era of National Systematic Surveys (1925–1940)

Nations began mapping specific regions. The German Meteor expedition (1925–1927) was pivotal, using acoustic sounders to map the South Atlantic and providing the first detailed look at vertical ocean structure.

4. Era of New Methods (1947–1956)

Post-WWII technology introduced seismic surveys and sophisticated electronic instruments, leading to the discovery of mid-ocean ridges and improved seafloor mapping.

5. Era of International Cooperation (1957–1978)

The International Geophysical Year (1957–58) spurred global collaboration. Large-scale experiments like MODE (Mid-Ocean Dynamics Experiment) shifted the focus toward ocean eddies and variability.

6. Era of Satellites (1978–1995)

Missions like Seasat (1978) and Topex/Poseidon (1992) allowed for synoptic, global views of sea surface height and temperature, transforming our understanding of planetary-scale circulation.

7. Era of Earth System Science (1995–Present)

Oceanography is now integrated into global climate models. The focus is on the ocean's role in the carbon cycle, heat storage, and biological productivity on a planetary scale.

IV. Scientific Milestones

The transition from description to theory was marked by several critical insights summarized by Stewart:

  • 1735 (Hadley): Proposed the effect of Earth's rotation on winds.
  • 1775 (Laplace): Developed the first comprehensive theory of tides.
  • 1847 (Maury): Published the first systematic charts of ocean winds and currents.
  • 1902 (Ekman): Explained how wind drives surface currents and the "Ekman Spiral" (Jan 8 Context).
  • 1947 (Sverdrup): Published the wind-driven theory of ocean circulation.
  • 1948 (Stommel): Explained the westward intensification of ocean currents (why the Gulf Stream is strong).

V. The Challenge of Measurement

A central theme of the Jan 7 lecture is the difficulty of oceanic measurement compared to the atmosphere. Stewart supports this, stating that sampling errors are the largest source of error in oceanography.

Why Ocean Measurement is Hard:

  • Opacity: Unlike the atmosphere, seawater is opaque to electromagnetic radiation. We cannot use radar or traditional satellites to "see" deep into the water; we must physically go there or use sound.
  • Pressure: Instruments must survive massive pressures (1 decibar for every meter depth).
  • Corrosion: The salt environment is hostile to electronics and mechanical parts.
  • Aliasing: Because we can't be everywhere at once, infrequent sampling can lead to a "misleading conceptual picture" (Stewart Section 2.6).
The Salinity Problem: Conductivity measurements (used to determine salinity) are highly sensitive to temperature. Therefore, an error in temperature propagates into an error in density calculations.

VI. Physical Dynamics & Framing

In the Jan 8 lecture, the focus shifts to how we frame the ocean using the laws of physics. We treat the ocean as a fluid in motion, governed by Newton's Second Law ($F=ma$). This requires a rigorous definition of how we track motion.

The Two Frames of Reference

Fluid motion is uniquely complex because the "particles" we observe are constantly moving. We use two mathematical frameworks to address this:

1. Eulerian Framework

The Fixed Perspective: You stand on a bridge and watch the water flow beneath you. You measure properties at a fixed position $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$.

Instrument: Current Meter Mooring.

2. Lagrangian Framework

The Moving Perspective: You jump into a boat and drift with the current. You measure properties of the same parcel of water as it moves.

Instrument: Argo Float / Drifting Buoy.

Mathematical Link: The Material Derivative

To apply Newton's Laws, we need to know the acceleration of a water parcel (Lagrangian), but our models and equations are often written for fixed points in space (Eulerian). We bridge this gap using the Material Derivative (or Substantial Derivative), denoted as $\frac{D}{Dt}$.

If $\phi$ represents a property of the fluid (such as Temperature $T$ or Salinity $S$), the total rate of change of that property for a moving parcel is given by:

$$\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\phi$$

Expanding this in Cartesian coordinates ($x, y, z$) with velocity components $(u, v, w)$:

$$\frac{D\phi}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$$

Physical Interpretation:

  • Local Change ($\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}$): How the property changes at a fixed spot (e.g., the sun warming the water while you stay still).
  • Advection ($(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\phi$): The change occurring because the current is carrying water with different properties past you. As discussed in the Jan 8 lecture, even if the temperature at every spot is constant in time ($\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = 0$), a thermometer on a moving drifter will record a change if it moves from a warm area to a cold area.

Conservation of Momentum

When we apply this to the velocity vector itself ($\phi = \mathbf{u}$), we get the acceleration term used in the Navier-Stokes equations for the ocean:

$$\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$$

This "Sweet Spot" of research—integrating these complex dynamics with real-world observations—is the goal of modern Physical Oceanography.

VII. Modern Methodology

As we conclude this overview, it's important to recognize the shift in how we observe the ocean today, as noted in the final sections of the Stewart PDF.

  • The Decline of Ship-Based Data: Fewer observations now come from ships. Instead, we rely on autonomous instruments (like Argo floats) and satellites.
  • Data Availability: Oceanographers rely more on large datasets produced by others. This requires an understanding of the limitations and errors inherent in those datasets.
  • Numerical Modeling: Our descriptive knowledge is now sufficient for time-averaged mean circulation, but our current work is focused on variability and climate-scale shifts.
